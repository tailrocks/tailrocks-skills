# Section 8: Punctuation and Word Count (Rules 8.1–8.7)

**Standard Reference:** ASD-STE100, Issue 9 (2025-01-15)  
**Part 1 — Writing Rules:** Section 8 (Punctuation and Word Count, Rules 8.1–8.7)

---

## 1. Overview and Scope

Punctuation in ASD-STE100 serves a precise structural and disambiguating function. It defines clause boundaries, connects tightly related terms, sets off supplementary information, and establishes the limits for sentence word counts.

Unlike literary English, where punctuation is often a matter of stylistic preference or vocal rhythm, STE treats punctuation as a functional mechanism to prevent reader misinterpretation and facilitate automated translation.

Section 8 establishes:
- The categorical ban on the semicolon (Rule 8.1).
- Precise criteria for using hyphens in compound terms (Rule 8.2).
- The seven strictly permitted uses of parentheses (Rule 8.3).
- Word-counting mechanics for vertical lists (Rule 8.4).
- Word-counting mechanics for parenthetical text (Rule 8.5).
- The seven special word-counting groups that count as a single word (Rule 8.6).
- Word-counting rules for hyphenated terms (Rule 8.7).

---

## 2. Rule 8.1: Prohibition of Semicolons

> **Rule 8.1:** You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;).

### 2.1 Principles and Rationale
The semicolon (;) is completely forbidden in ASD-STE100 prose. Semicolons encourage authors to link independent clauses into excessively long, complex compound sentences. Furthermore, semicolons are frequently misused and poorly understood by non-native speakers.

Whenever a semicolon would appear in standard English:
> **MANDATE:** Replace the semicolon with a full stop (period) and write **two separate, independent sentences**.

#### Contrastive Examples (Rule 8.1):
- **Non-STE:**  
  `(1) Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones.`  
  **STE:**  
  `(1) Examine the removed parts for damage.`  
  `(2) Replace the damaged part(s).`  
  *(Also resolves the [Rule 5.2](procedures.md#3-rule-52-one-instruction-per-sentence) violation by creating two separate work steps).*
- **Non-STE:**  
  `The battery is not user-replaceable; it can only be replaced by an approved service station.`  
  **STE:**  
  `Users cannot replace the battery. Only specialists at approved service stations can replace it.`

### 2.2 Standard Punctuation and House-Style Em-Dash Bans
STE permits standard English punctuation marks: periods, commas, colons, question marks, quotation marks, parentheses, and hyphens. 

> [!NOTE]
> Some corporate style guides and authoring tools enforce a blanket ban on em-dashes (—). This is a **company or project house-style rule**, not an ASD-STE100 requirement. STE distinguishes hyphens from dashes but does not ban punctuation marks other than the semicolon.

For general punctuation rules not explicitly covered by STE, the standard refers writers to authoritative external style manuals:
- *The Chicago Manual of Style* (18th ed., 2024)
- *The Gregg Reference Manual* (10th ed., 2011)
- *U.S. Government Publishing Office Style Manual* (2020)
- *Practical English Usage* (Swan, 4th ed., 2017)
- *Punctuation Matters* (Kirkman, 2006)

## 3. Rule 8.2: Use of Hyphens

> **Rule 8.2:** Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.

### 3.1 Principles and Disambiguation
A hyphen connects two or more words or parts of words. In technical nouns and modifiers, hyphens indicate direct semantic attachment, preventing the reader from misassociating modifiers with adjacent nouns.

### 3.2 Five Permitted Categories of Hyphenated Terms
The standard specifies five distinct morphological and syntactic categories where hyphens are required:

#### 1. Compound Modifiers Preceding a Noun (Phrasal Adjectives)
When two or more words function together as a single adjective before a noun:
- `low-altitude flight`
- `high-pressure chamber`
- `air-conditioned compartment`
- `transmitter-receiver system`
- `quick-release fastener`
- `clamshell-type flap`
- `eighteen-inch monitor`
- `cast-aluminum bracket`
- `three-to-one ratio`
- `trial-and-error method`
- `air-to-air refueling`
- `soap-and-water solution`
- `up-to-date information`
- `run-on torque`
- `break-away torque`
- `cut-in speed`
- `in-flight entertainment system`
- `stiff-bristled brush`
- `fire-resistant material`
- `self-sealing hose`

#### 2. Two-Word Fractions and Numbers
Compound numbers and written fractions:
- `forty-seven`
- `ninety-ninth`
- `one hundred and sixty-two`
- `three-sixteenths`
- `one thirty-second`

#### 3. Letters/Numbers with Nouns Indicating Shape or Configuration
Terms combining an alphanumeric character or number with a noun to describe geometry or specification:
- `L-shaped bracket`
- `O-ring`
- `T-shirt`
- `U-beam`
- `Y-coupling`
- `V-band clamp`
- `3-prong connector`
- `180-grit abrasive cloth`

#### 4. Verbs Compounded with Nouns or Other Parts of Speech
Verbs formed by prefixing a noun or other part of speech:
- `die-cast`
- `arc-weld`
- `fusion-bond`
- `stop-drill`
- `vacuum-pack`
- `heat-treat`
- `jump-start`
- `air-condition`
- `short-circuit`
- `fast-forward`
- `cold-roll`
- `dry-clean`
- `blow-dry`

#### 5. Prefixes Ending in a Vowel before a Root Starting with a Vowel
To prevent pronunciation ambiguity or double-vowel confusion:
- `pre-amplifier`
- `de-icing`
- `anti-icing`
- `pre-engage`

### 3.3 Distinction: Hyphen vs. Dash vs. Range vs. Minus
A **hyphen** (-) connects words. It is functionally distinct from:
- An **en-dash / em-dash** (– / —), which indicates pauses, parenthesis, or clause separation.
- A **range indicator** (e.g., `10–20 mm` or STE `10 to 20 mm`).
- A **mathematical minus sign** ($-$) in formulas.
- A **command-line flag** (e.g., `--verbose`, `-rf`).

## 4. Rule 8.3: The Seven Permitted Uses of Parentheses

> **Rule 8.3:** You can use parentheses:  
>  - To make references to illustrations or text  
>  - To include letters or numbers that identify items on an illustration or in a text  
>  - To identify the work steps in a procedure  
>  - To include abbreviations  
>  - To give the singular and plural forms of a noun at the same time  
>  - To explain words or a part of a sentence  
>  - To include an alternative.

Parentheses may **only** be used for these seven specific functions:

### 1. References to Illustrations or Text
Directing the reader to an external figure, table, or section:
- `Remove the valve (10, Figure 1).`
- `Install the cover (refer to paragraphs 2 thru 5).`

### 2. Item Callout Identifiers (Letters or Numbers)
Cross-referencing parts to callout numbers on an engineering schematic:
- `Disconnect the hoses (2) and (12) from the suction ejector (8).`
- `Remove the nuts (74), the washers (76), the bolts (68), the seals (70), and the bonding straps (72).`

### 3. Work Step Identification in Procedures
Enclosing step numbers or sub-step letters:
- `(1) Install the locking cap (4) on the body (8).`
- `(2) Safety the locking cap (4) with the cotter pin (5).`
- `(3) Install a new retaining ring (6).`

### 4. Introducing Abbreviations or Acronyms
Defining an acronym or initialism upon its initial appearance:
- `A Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) is a flat-panel display that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals.`

### 5. Plural Alternatives (Dual Number)
Indicating that an instruction applies to either singular or plural quantities without knowing the exact count in advance:
- `A. Before you do the test(s), install the component(s).`
- `B. Do the applicable test(s).`

### 6. Explanatory Clarifications
Briefly clarifying or defining a word, switch state, or parameter:
- `Increase the pressure slowly (not more than 10 psi each minute).`
- `Make sure that the BLEED pushbutton switch is released (the ON legend is off).`

### 7. Explicit Alternatives
Presenting symmetric bilateral options (e.g., left vs. right installations):
- `Open the left (right) access panel L42 (R42).`

## 5. Rule 8.4: Word Counting in Vertical Lists

> **Rule 8.4:** In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence.

### 5.1 The Independent Budget Principle
In a vertical list:
1. The **lead-in sentence** (ending in a colon) is evaluated as a complete sentence and receives its own word-count budget:
   - Max **20 words** for procedural lead-ins ([Rule 5.1](procedures.md#2-rule-51-maximum-sentence-length-in-procedures)).
   - Max **25 words** for descriptive lead-ins ([Rule 6.3](descriptions.md#4-rule-63-maximum-sentence-length-in-descriptions)).
2. **Each list item** is treated as a separate sentence unit for word count:
   - Max **20 words** per procedural list item.
   - Max **25 words** per descriptive list item.

### 5.2 Example: Fire Extinguisher Locations (Rule 8.4)

```markdown
To extinguish a possible fire, portable fire extinguishers are installed in these areas:  (13 words)
- The cockpit                                                                            (2 words)
- The cabin                                                                              (2 words)
- The cabin sub-compartment                                                              (3 words)
- The crew rest compartment.                                                             (4 words)
```

*Analysis:*
- The lead-in sentence contains 13 words ($\le$ 25 words).
- Each list item contains 2, 2, 3, and 4 words ($\le$ 25 words).
- All units are fully compliant.

### 5.3 Important Distinction: Colons vs. Vertical Lists
A colon does not automatically create a vertical list:
- A colon followed by inline prose within a standard paragraph does **not** reset the word count.
- Rule 8.4 applies strictly to **formatted vertical lists** where items appear on separate physical lines with bullets, dashes, or step markers.

## 6. Rule 8.5: Word Counting with Parentheses

> **Rule 8.5:** When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence.

### 6.1 Two-Tier Counting Mechanics
Parenthetical expressions are evaluated at two distinct levels:
1. **External Count (Host Sentence):** In the containing sentence, the entire parenthetical expression (regardless of length) is counted as **exactly one word**.
2. **Internal Count (Parenthetical Sentence):** The text enclosed within the parentheses forms its own distinct syntactic unit and must independently obey the sentence length limit (max 20 words for procedures, max 25 words for descriptions).

#### Example (Rule 8.5):
> `Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released (the EMER legend is off).`

*Count Breakdown:*
- **Host sentence:** `Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released [PARENTICAL].`  
  Count: 9 words + 1 word for parenthetical text = **10 words** (Compliant with Rule 5.1).
- **Internal parenthetical unit:** `the EMER legend is off`  
  Count: 5 words (Compliant with Rule 5.1 / Rule 6.3).

### 6.2 Identifiers and Abbreviations in Parentheses
Alphanumeric item callouts and abbreviations enclosed in parentheses count as **one word**:
- `Remove the safety pin (10).` → **5 words** (`Remove` [1], `the` [2], `safety` [3], `pin` [4], `(10)` [5]).
- `Installation of a Business Class (B/C) Seat` → **7 words** (`Installation` [1], `of` [2], `a` [3], `Business` [4], `Class` [5], `(B/C)` [6], `Seat` [7]).
- `Hardware and Software Configuration Check of the In-Flight Entertainment (IFE) System` → **11 words** (`(IFE)` counts as 1 word).

## 7. Rule 8.6: The Seven Special Counting Groups (1 Word Each)

> **Rule 8.6:** Count each of these elements as one word:  
>  - Numbers  
>  - Numbers together with units of measurement  
>  - Abbreviations  
>  - Alphanumeric identifiers  
>  - Quoted text  
>  - Titles, headings, and text on placards and labels  
>  - Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities.

When evaluating sentence length against Rules 5.1 and 6.3, each of the following seven elements counts as **one word**:

### 1. Numbers (Digits or Spelled Out)
- Digits: `13`, `16`, `1000` → **1 word each**.
- Spelled-out compound numbers: `twenty-one`, `forty-seven` → **1 word each**.
- *Example:* `Do steps 13 thru 16 a minimum of three times.` → **10 words** (`13` = 1, `16` = 1, `three` = 1).
- *Example:* `The spar box has twenty-one ribs.` → **6 words** (`twenty-one` = 1).
- **EXCLUSION:** Paragraph numbers and work step labels (e.g., `(1)`, `A.`, `Step 4`) belong to the document numbering structure and are **excluded entirely from the sentence word count**.

### 2. Numbers Combined with Units of Measurement
When a number is immediately followed by its unit of measurement (symbol or full word), including tolerances, the entire combination counts as **one word**:
- `10 °C` or `10 degrees Celsius` → **1 word**
- `20 kg` or `20 kilograms` → **1 word**
- `10 Ω` or `10 ohms` → **1 word**
- `60 psi` → **1 word**
- `4.20 Nm` → **1 word**
- `6 mm` or `0.24 in` → **1 word**
- `0.05 mm ± 0.01 mm` → **1 word**
- *Example:* `The unit weighs 20 kilograms.` → **4 words** (`The` [1], `unit` [2], `weighs` [3], `20 kilograms` [4]).

### 3. Abbreviations, Acronyms, and Initialisms
Every standard abbreviation, acronym, or initialism counts as **one word**:
- `a.m.` (counted together with its time number: `10 a.m.` = 1 word)
- `NASA` (acronym) → **1 word**
- `VPN` (initialism) → **1 word**
- `APU`, `RAM`, `LCD`, `CSD` → **1 word each**
- *Example:* `During this safety check, obey NASA protocols.` → **7 words** (`NASA` = 1).

### 4. Alphanumeric Identifiers and Part Numbers
Combinations of letters, punctuation, and digits designating parts, assemblies, IP addresses, or locations count as **one word**:
- `No. 1` → **1 word**
- `36L7` → **1 word**
- `MS20470AD4-4` → **1 word**
- `192.168.1.10` → **1 word**
- `L42` / `R42` → **1 word each**
- *Example:* `Examine the No. 1 bearing installation.` → **5 words** (`No. 1` = 1).
- *Example:* `Tag circuit breaker 36L7.` → **4 words** (`36L7` = 1).

### 5. Quoted Text and Mathematical Formulas
Text enclosed in quotation marks (or distinguished by capitalization or font as quoted UI/hardware labels) counts as **one word**. Mathematical formulas also count as **one word**:
- `"Service Overview"` → **1 word**
- `SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST` (when representing an engraved panel switch legend) → **1 word**
- `C = (A - B) - 0.063 mm` → **1 word** (mathematical formula)
- *Example:* `Touch the "Service Overview" arrow to select the function page.` → **9 words** (`"Service Overview"` = 1).
- *Example:* `Release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch.` → **4 words** (`SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST` = 1).

### 6. Fixed Titles, Headings, Placards, and Hardware Labels
Immutable official titles of manuals, specifications, data modules, placards, and shipping labels count as **one word**:
- `Structural Repair Manual` (manual title) → **1 word**
- `Testing and Fault Isolation` (chapter heading) → **1 word**
- `Requirements after Job Completion` (data module heading) → **1 word**
- Placard text: `"Interior hazards exist to such a degree that interior operations may be conducted only after full examination, and with extreme caution."` → **1 word**
- Shipping label: `"FRAGILE - Please handle with care."` → **1 word**
- *Example:* `Refer to Testing and Fault Isolation, page block 1001.` → **6 words** (`Refer` [1], `to` [2], `Testing and Fault Isolation` [3], `page` [4], `block` [5], `1001` [6]).

### 7. Proper Nouns (Individuals, Organizations, Geopolitical Entities)
Multi-word proper names of persons, corporate bodies, agencies, and nations count as **one word**:
- `George Washington` (individual) → **1 word**
- `Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe` (organization) → **1 word**
- `United States of America` (geopolitical entity) → **1 word**
- `Republic of Ireland` → **1 word**
- *Example:* `The first president of the United States of America was George Washington.` → **8 words** (`The` [1], `first` [2], `president` [3], `of` [4], `the` [5], `United States of America` [6], `was` [7], `George Washington` [8]).
- *Example:* `The owner of STE is the Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe.` → **7 words** (`The` [1], `owner` [2], `of` [3], `STE` [4], `is` [5], `the` [6], `Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe` [7]).

## 8. Rule 8.7: Hyphenated Words Count as One Word

> **Rule 8.7:** Hyphenated words count as one word.

### 8.1 Principles and Application
Compound words joined by hyphens according to Rule 8.2 count as **one word**:
- Pre-nominal compound adjectives: `soap-and-water solution` → `soap-and-water` = 1 word; `Clean the surface with a soap-and-water solution.` = **7 words** (`Clean` [1], `the` [2], `surface` [3], `with` [4], `a` [5], `soap-and-water` [6], `solution` [7]).
- Idiomatic compound adjectives: `trial-and-error method` → `trial-and-error` = 1 word; `Use the trial-and-error method.` = **4 words** (`Use` [1], `the` [2], `trial-and-error` [3], `method` [4]).

### 8.2 Hyphenated Technical Noun Clusters
When hyphens are used to clarify the internal grouping of complex technical nouns (subject to Rule 2.2 noun cluster limits):
- `Cutoff-switch power connection` → **3 words** (`Cutoff-switch` [1], `power` [2], `connection` [3]).
- `Main-gear-door retraction-winch handle` → **3 words** (`Main-gear-door` [1], `retraction-winch` [2], `handle` [3]).

> [!WARNING]
> Writers may not artificially insert hyphens between random words to bypass the 20-word or 25-word sentence limit. Only genuine, structurally valid compound modifiers and technical terms conforming to Rule 8.2 qualify as single words.
